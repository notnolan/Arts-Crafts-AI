- "Art is the expression of man's pleasure in labour" (p. i)
- The gothic has both external fomrms and internal elements
	- "Its elements are certain mental tendencies of the builders, legibly expressed in itl as fancifulness, love of variety, love of richness, & such others." (p. 5)
	- "Its external forms are painted arches, vaulted roofs, etc." (p. 5)
	- "Unless both the elements & the firms are there, we have no right to call the style Gothic." (p. 5)
- Ruskin defines this split as power and form. Power refers to the internal elements of the Gothic, while form refers to the external attributes. (p. 5)
- Ruskin does not make a distinction between gothic and not gothic; instead, he clarifies that it is a spectrum. (p. 6)
	- "if we withdraw one of its mental elements from the gothic style, it is only a little less Gothic than it was before." (p. 6)
- Description of relationship between technical acumen and knowledge of the field
	- "The Greek master workman was far advanced in knowledge and power above the Assyrian or Egyptian. Neither he nor those for whom he worked could endure the appearance of imperfection in anything; and, therefore, what ornament he appointed to be done by those beneath him was composed of mere geometrical forms, balls, ridges, and perfectly symmetrical foliage, which could be executed with absolute precision by line and rule, and were as perfect in their way, when completed, as his own figure sculpture. The Assyrian and Egyptian, on the contrary, less cognisant of accurate form in anything, were content to allow their figure sculptures to be executed by inferior workmen, but lowered the method of its treatment to a standard which every workman could reach, and then trained him by discipline so rigid, that there was no chance of his falling beneath the standard appointed." (p. 13)
	- The renaissance is also used to capture this relationship
		- "The third kind of ornament, the Renaissance, is that in which the inferior detail becomes principal, executor of every minor portion being required to exhibit skill and possess knowledge as great as that which is possessed by the master of the design." (p. 14)
		- However, Ruskin derides this approach. He claims the practice is "well-educated imbecility." (p. 14)
- He purports that Gothic school of thought accepts the "inferior labour" and "raises up a stately[...] whole." (p. 15)
- "For the best that is in them cannot manifest itself, but in company with much error." (p. 17)
	- "Understand this clearly: You can teach a man to draw a straight line, and to cut one; to strike a curved one, and to carve it; and to copy and carve any number of given lines or forms, with admireable speed and perfect precision; and you find hiswork perfect of its kind: but if you ask him to think about any of those forms, to consider if he cannot find any better in his own head, he stops; his execution becomes hesitating; he thinks wrong, ten to one he thinks wrong; ten to one he makes a mistake in the first touch he gives to his work as a thinking being. But you have made a man of him for all that. He was only a machine before, an animated tool." (p. 17)
- Again makes a delineation between skill and thought: "You must either make a tool of the creature, or a man of him" (p. 17)
- There's also an element here that more simple design is a product of efficiency and derision of human spirit, whereas the gothic captures imperfection but rich human creativity (p. 20).
